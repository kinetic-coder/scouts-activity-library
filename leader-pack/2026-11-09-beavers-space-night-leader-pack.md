# Earth and Solar System Mission

## Purpose
Explore the Solar System through a short, cooperative ordering challenge. Beavers identify the Sun, the eight planets and Pluto, then share what makes each world different.

## Observable objectives
- Put the eight planets in the correct order away from the Sun.
- Recognise that the Sun is a star and Pluto is a dwarf planet.
- Share or listen to one fact about a Solar System object.

## Kit
- 1 table, large enough to lay out the cut-outs in a line.
- 10 labelled Solar System cut-outs: Sun, Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune and Pluto. The name on each cut-out should be easy to read.
- 1 pair of clean gloves, used as an astronaut role prop.
- 1 printed copy of [Solar System order](#printable-solar-system-order) below.
- 1 printed copy of [Solar System fact cards](#printable-solar-system-fact-cards) below.

**Low-kit alternative:** write the ten names on large slips of paper instead of using cut-outs. If gloves are unavailable, nominate an “astronaut” without the prop.

## Setup
1. Put the table where every Beaver can reach it safely. Put the named cut-outs in a mixed-up pile at one end.
2. Print the order sheet and fact cards below. Keep the order sheet hidden until the check; cut apart the fact cards if leaders or Beavers will each hold one.
3. Explain that the Sun is a star. There are eight planets; Pluto comes after Neptune but is a **dwarf planet**, not a ninth planet.

## Instructions
1. Give one Beaver the gloves and call them the astronaut. They choose a cut-out, read the name (or ask for help), and place it on the table. Pass the gloves to the next astronaut after each turn.
2. As a team, arrange the objects in order away from the Sun. Use spoken clues and give everyone a chance to contribute. Avoid a speed race.
3. Reveal the printable order sheet and check together: Sun, Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, Pluto.
4. Read one or more relevant fact-card bullets as each cut-out is checked. Ask the team to choose one fact they found surprising.
5. Recap: the Sun is a star, Earth is our home planet, and Pluto is a dwarf planet. Return the cut-outs, gloves and papers to the kit box.

## Discussion prompts
- Which object gives light and heat to the Solar System?
- Which planet is our home? What helps living things survive here?
- Which worlds are rocky, and which are giant worlds made mostly of gas or ice?
- Why do we call Pluto a dwarf planet?

## Accessibility and adaptations
- Pair confident readers with anyone who would like help, and read labels and fact cards aloud.
- Let a Beaver point, give a verbal direction, hold a fact card, or be the order checker instead of handling a cut-out.
- Use a high-contrast, large-print order sheet and large cut-outs; keep the table accessible from a seated position.
- Reduce to Sun, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn and Pluto first, then add the remaining planets if the group needs a simpler challenge.

## Printable Solar System fact cards

Print this section, or cut the headings and bullets into cards. Use the facts as prompts rather than asking Beavers to memorise all of them.

### Sun — star
- The Sun is the star at the centre of our Solar System.
- It gives Earth light and heat.
- The planets travel around the Sun.

### Mercury — planet 1
- Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun.
- It is the smallest planet in our Solar System.
- It is a rocky planet with lots of craters.

### Venus — planet 2
- Venus is the second planet from the Sun.
- It is covered by thick clouds.
- It is about the same size as Earth.

### Earth — planet 3
- Earth is the third planet from the Sun.
- It is our home planet.
- Earth has oceans of liquid water and air that living things can use.

### Mars — planet 4
- Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun.
- It is often called the Red Planet because of rusty material in its soil.
- Mars is a rocky planet.

### Jupiter — planet 5
- Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun.
- It is the biggest planet in our Solar System.
- Jupiter is a gas giant, so it does not have a solid surface to stand on.

### Saturn — planet 6
- Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun.
- It is the second-largest planet in our Solar System.
- Saturn has bright rings made mostly of ice and rock pieces.

### Uranus — planet 7
- Uranus is the seventh planet from the Sun.
- It is an ice giant.
- Uranus spins on its side compared with most planets.

### Neptune — planet 8
- Neptune is the eighth and farthest planet from the Sun.
- It is an ice giant.
- Neptune has very fast winds.

### Pluto — dwarf planet
- Pluto is a dwarf planet beyond Neptune.
- It is smaller than Earth’s Moon.
- Pluto takes about 248 Earth years to travel once around the Sun.

# Robot Arm Relay Challenge

## Purpose and observable objectives
Beavers use a grabber carefully to move objects as a team, practising control and explaining that astronauts use robotic tools such as Canadarm2 on the ISS.

## Kit
- Picker/grabber tools, ideally one per Beaver or enough to share fairly.
- Six lightweight plastic balls large enough not to pose a choking hazard.
- Two buckets: one start bucket with all balls and one empty destination bucket.
- Clean, comfortable non-latex gloves for every Beaver.
- Timer, pen and [printable score sheet](#printable-score-sheet).

**Low-kit alternative:** use clean kitchen tongs and rolled socks/beanbags large enough to handle safely. If a Beaver cannot use gloves or a grabber, offer timer, scorekeeper, caller or an adapted transfer role.

## Setup
No table is required. Put buckets a safe walking distance apart with a clear route. Inspect grabbers for dirt, sharp edges and pinch points. Write each team's chosen name on the score sheet before it arrives.

## Procedure (11 minutes)
1. **Explain and demonstrate — 1–2 minutes.** Demonstrate picking up one ball while wearing gloves. Explain that space robotic arms move equipment carefully; accuracy matters as well as speed.
2. **Relay — up to 8–9 minutes.** One Beaver picks up one ball, walks to the destination bucket and drops it in. The next Beaver then takes a turn. Beavers walk; they do not run, throw or kick balls.
3. **Dropped-ball rule.** If a ball is dropped, return that ball to the original bucket and restart that turn.
4. **Record and repeat.** Stop the timer when every ball is in the destination bucket. Record the time, reset, and repeat complete attempts while time allows. The fastest completed attempt is the score; do not begin an attempt that cannot finish and be tidied safely.
5. **Finish — final minute.** Put tools down, record the best score and arrange handwashing with soap and water. If this cannot happen safely inside the rotation, the session leader must supervise it immediately after the station.

## Accessibility and adaptations
Use a shorter route, a larger/lighter object or a partner as needed. A Beaver can hold the bucket steady, time, record, encourage, or take a stationary transfer role. Celebrate careful teamwork rather than ranking individuals.

## Safety controls (draft for leader review)
Keep the route clear and use soft, non-chokable objects. Inspect and clean tools; no running; keep fingers clear of grabber mechanisms. Confirm handwashing access and supervision. The responsible leader must review this draft with group risk procedures and individual adjustments before use.

## Tidy and reset
Return balls to the start bucket, clean/store tools and gloves, and retain the score sheet for the session recap.

# Design Your Own Space Rocket

## Purpose and observable objectives
Teams design one labelled rocket for an imagined mission. Beavers can name at least one rocket part and explain one feature that helps their mission.

## Kit
- One table; one large sheet of paper per team (A3 if available).
- Coloured pencils, felt-tip pens or crayons.
- Optional clean cardboard tubes, small boxes, tape, glue sticks and child-safe scissors.
- Timer and this instruction sheet.

**Low-kit alternative:** paper and pencils are sufficient. The shared drawing is required; a recycled-material model is optional.

## Setup
Put paper and drawing materials on the table. Inspect optional recycled materials for staples or sharp edges and put scissors with the leader. Keep finished drawings for the end-of-evening show-and-tell.

## Procedure (11 minutes)
1. **Introduce rockets — 2 minutes.** Explain that rockets carry people, spacecraft or equipment into space. Briefly introduce: a nose cone/payload fairing; engines and fuel tanks; stages or boosters; a crew capsule or cargo section; and a return or landing system. Different rockets use different combinations.
2. **Choose a mission — 1 minute.** The team chooses the Moon, Mars, Pluto or another destination, and what the rocket carries.
3. **Draw and label — 6 minutes.** Make one shared drawing with the team name, an optional rocket name, destination/mission and labelled parts. Make sure everyone can contribute. Only add a simple model if the drawing is complete and time/materials allow.
4. **Prepare a share — 1 minute.** Choose one feature to explain and why it helps the mission.
5. **Tidy — 1 minute.** Put away materials and leave the labelled drawing for the later show-and-tell.

## Prompts
- How will astronauts breathe and live on a long journey?
- Does the rocket carry a rover, people or a probe?
- Does anything need to return safely to Earth?

## Accessibility and adaptations
Offer a choice of drawing, dictating labels, holding materials, choosing mission ideas or presenting. Use thick pens, pre-drawn outlines or adult scribing where helpful. Do not make a model a condition of taking part.

## Safety controls (draft for leader review)
Use only clean materials. An adult supervises scissors and removes sharp/damaged items; no aerosols, fuels or launches. Keep glue/tape use age-appropriate and follow group procedures. The responsible leader must review this draft with venue conditions and individual adjustments before use.

## Tidy and reset
Store drawings flat for show-and-tell; count scissors and return all materials to the kit box.

# Space Station Charades: Life as an Astronaut

## Purpose and observable objectives
Beavers mime daily life on the International Space Station (ISS) and identify one way microgravity changes a familiar task.

## Kit and setup
Print or quietly read the six [private prompt cards](#private-prompt-cards). Use a timer and a clear open area; no table, gloves, toothbrushes, food or specialist equipment are needed.

## Procedure (11 minutes)
1. **Introduce — 1 minute.** Explain that the ISS is a laboratory orbiting Earth. Astronauts and loose objects seem to float because they experience microgravity.
2. **Six charades — about 6 minutes.** Quietly show/read one card to one Beaver at a time. They act without speaking; the group guesses. After about 40–45 seconds, give the leader fact. Everyone has a turn; invite volunteers for extra cards in smaller groups.
3. **Discuss or repeat — 3 minutes.** Repeat favourites or ask how the activity differs on Earth.
4. **Wrap up — 1 minute.** Ask which everyday task would be hardest in space, then prepare to rotate.

## Accessibility and adaptations
Allow a Beaver to choose, narrate, make sound effects, draw a clue, hold a card, guess, or partner with an adult instead of acting. Read cards aloud and allow extra thinking time.

## Safety controls (draft for leader review)
Keep the acting area clear. Mime only: no climbing, jumping from furniture, straps, bags over heads, restricted movement or physical contact. The responsible leader must review this draft with venue and individual needs before use.

## Tidy and reset
Collect and keep the cards private, ready for the next group.

## Badge mapping
Potential support for requirement 3 of the [Beaver Space Activity Badge](https://www.scouts.org.uk/beavers/activity-badges/space/): find out what living on the ISS is like, including daily tasks. It needs leader-confirmed evidence and does not award a badge by itself.

## Private prompt cards

### 1. Sleeping in space
**Actor (read quietly):** Climb into a sleeping bag, fasten it to a wall, close your eyes and float slightly.  
**Leader fact:** Astronauts secure sleeping bags in their sleeping quarters so they do not drift around.

### 2. Washing your hair
**Actor (read quietly):** Use a little shampoo, rub your hair, look for a shower, then use a towel.  
**Leader fact:** Astronauts use rinseless shampoo and small amounts of water; water forms floating blobs in microgravity.

### 3. Going to the toilet
**Actor (read quietly):** Float to the toilet, hold handles, position yourself carefully and fasten in place.  
**Leader fact:** Space toilets use moving air and suction because gravity does not pull waste down. Keep the mime silly but age-appropriate.

### 4. Eating dinner
**Actor (read quietly):** Open a packet, eat with a spoon, catch food that floats away, then take another bite.  
**Leader fact:** Astronauts use packaged food and secure items so they do not drift away.

### 5. Exercising in space
**Actor (read quietly):** Step onto a treadmill, attach a harness and run on the spot, then float upward.  
**Leader fact:** ISS astronauts exercise for around two hours daily to protect muscles and bones; a harness holds them to the treadmill.

### 6. Repairing the space station
**Actor (read quietly):** Pretend to wear a spacesuit and helmet, open an airlock, hold on and tighten a loose bolt with an imaginary tool.  
**Leader fact:** During spacewalks, astronauts use spacesuits for oxygen and protection and remain attached safely to the station.
